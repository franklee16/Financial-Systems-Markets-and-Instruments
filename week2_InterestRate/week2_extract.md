## Slide 4  [layout: Blank]
 Interest Rate Basics
    - Nominal interest rates i
      - the interest rates actually observed in financial markets (bonds or loans)
    - Real risk-free rate (RFR)
      - the true increase in purchasing power you earn on a risk-free investment
    - What causes differences in nominal and real interest rates?
 Economist Irving Fisher first postulated that nominal interest rates (i) contain the compensation for expected inflation (IP):
  - Nominal risk-free rate ≈ Real risk-free rate + Expected inflation
  - i ≈ RFR+E(IP)
  - The exact formula for Fisher equation is 1+i = (1+RFR)*(1+E(IP))
  [NOTES: RFR measures society’s relative time preference for consuming today rather than tomorrow.]

## Slide 5  [layout: Blank]
 Practical Example
 Suppose:
 Nominal interest rate (10-year Treasury yield) = 5%
 Expected inflation = 3%
 Then: Real risk-free rate ≈ 5% − 3% = 2% (Exact: (1.05 / 1.03) − 1 ≈ 1.94%)
 → Your money grows 5% in dollar terms, but after inflation erodes purchasing power by 3%, your real wealth only increases by ~2%.
 In valuation (e.g., DCF, CAPM), people usually use the nominal risk-free rate together with nominal cash flows
 However, when working with real cash flows (inflation-adjusted), the real risk-free rate is the consistent choice
 In short:
 Nominal = what you actually see and get paid
 Real = what really matters for long-term wealth growth after inflation

## Slide 8  [layout: Blank]
 Loanable Funds Theory
 Loanable funds theory explains interest rate levels and interest rate movements
  - Views level of interest rates in financial markets as a result of the supply of and demand for loanable funds
  - Interest rate is the price of loanable funds
  - Categorizes financial market participants – e.g., consumers, businesses, governments, and foreign participants – as net suppliers or demanders of funds
  - The equilibrium interest rate is where the quantity of funds supplied equals the quantity demanded
  [NOTES: Ignore inflation for the moment]

## Slide 10  [layout: Blank]
 Supply of Loanable Funds
 Supply comes mainly from savings — funds not consumed today but set aside for future use
 Main sources:
 Household savings
 Business retained earnings
 Government budget surplus (public saving)
 Foreign capital inflows (net capital inflow in open economies)
 Supply curve: Upward-sloping → Higher real interest rates → greater incentive to save (postpone consumption) → more funds supplied
  [NOTES: Ignore inflation for the moment]

## Slide 11  [layout: Blank]
 Demand of Loanable Funds
 Demand comes from borrowers who want funds for investment (capital goods, business expansion) or other purposes (e.g., government deficits, consumer durables)
 Main sources:
 Firms borrowing for physical capital (machinery, factories, R&D) — most important
 Households borrowing for big-ticket items (homes, cars)
 Government borrowing to finance budget deficits
 Demand curve: Downward-sloping → Higher real interest rates → borrowing becomes more expensive → fewer projects/investments are profitable → less funds demanded
  [NOTES: Ignore inflation for the moment]

## Slide 20  [layout: Blank]
 Determinants of Interest Rates for Individual Securities
 ij* = f(IP, RFR, DRPj, LRPj, SCPj, MPj)
 ij* = equilibrium nominal interest rate for a given security j
 Inflation (IP)
  - IP = [(CPIt+1) – (CPIt)]/(CPIt) x (100)
  - CPI: consumer price index, the price level of a basket of goods and services purchased by the typical urban consumer
 Real risk-free interest rate (RFR) and the Fisher equation
  - RFR = i – Expected (IP)
  - RFR measures society’s relative time preference for consuming today rather than tomorrow
  [NOTES: The actual Fisher Effect is given as (1+i) = (1+RFR)*(1+Expected(IP)); i=RFR + Expected(IP) + RFR*Expected(IP)]

 Default risk premium (DRP)
  - DRPj = ijt – iTt
    - ijt = interest rate on security j at time t
    - iTt = interest rate on similar maturity U.S. Treasury security at time t
  [IMAGE -> figures/slide21_img.png]
  [NOTES: DRPs: compensation for default risk, are countercyclical, and rise in periods of weak economic conditions]

## Slide 22  [layout: Blank]
 Determinants of Interest Rates for Individual Securities (cont’d)
 Liquidity risk premium (LRP)
  - Increases in required yields designed to offset the risk of not being able to sell the asset in timely fashion at fair value
 Special feature premium (SCP)
  - Taxability (Municipal bond is exempt from federal/state taxes)
  - Convertibility (Convertible bonds have lower yields)
  - Callability (Callable bonds have higher required yields)
 Maturity premium (MP)
  - Difference between yield on long- and short-term securities of the same characteristics
 Inflation and real risk-free rate are same for all securities, while DPR, LPR, SCP and MP are security specific
  [NOTES: Convertible bond: bondholder has the right to convert them to preferred or common stock at their choice
Callable bond: issuer has the right to call back the bond at pre-determined price

## Slide 25  [layout: Blank]
 Theories on the Term Structure of Interest Rates
 Several major theories explain why the yield curve takes different shapes—upward-sloping (normal), flat, downward-sloping (inverted), or humped—and what drives long-term vs. short-term rates
 Unbiased Expectations Theory (UET)
  - Long-term interest rates are purely the geometric average of current and expected future short-term interest rates
 Liquidity Premium Theory
  - Builds on pure expectations but adds a positive liquidity premium (or term premium) that increases with maturity
 Market Segmentation Theory
  - Markets for bonds of different maturities are completely separate (segmented); investors and borrowers have strict maturity preferences and do not substitute across maturities

## Slide 29  [layout: Blank]
 Liquidity Premium Theory
 Long-term interest rates are geometric averages of current and expected future short-term interest rates plus a liquidity premium that increase with maturity
  - Lt = liquidity premium for period t
  - L2 < L3 < …<LN
  - This theory implicitly assumes that investors prefer short-term securities over long-term securities, because they are more liquid and price is less sensitive to interest rate change

## Slide 31  [layout: Blank]
 Market Segmentation Theory
 Individual investors and FIs have specific maturity preferences
  - Money market funds prefer short-term Treasury bonds
  - Insurance companies and pension funds prefer longer maturity bonds
 Yields in each maturity segment are determined independently by supply and demand within that segment
 Investors and borrowers deviate from their preferred maturity segment only when adequately compensated to do so
 Long-term rate is unrelated to expected future short-term rate

## Slide 39  [layout: Blank]
 What are the Mechanisms Behind this Predictability?
 Main Mechanism: Expectations of Future Fed Rate Cuts Due to Economic Weakness
 Bond investors and the market as a whole price Treasuries based on expectations of future short-term interest rates (which the Federal Reserve heavily influences via the federal funds rate), plus a term premium for risk and inflation uncertainty
  - A upward-sloping yield curve means investors expect short-term rates to rise over time (or at least stay stable), typically during economic expansions when growth and inflation are anticipated to pick up
  - An inverted yield curve (downward-sloping) signals that investors expect short-term rates to fall significantly in the future
 Why would the market expect the Fed to cut rates aggressively? Usually because it anticipates economic slowdown or outright recession ahead

## Slide 40  [layout: Blank]
 Secondary Channel: Tight Monetary Policy and Credit Conditions
 Inversions often occur after the Fed has hiked short-term rates aggressively to fight inflation (raising short yields sharply while long yields rise less or fall due to expected future easing)
 This aggressive tightening can itself slow the economy by:
  - Raising borrowing costs for businesses and households
  - Squeezing bank profitability (banks borrow short-term and lend long-term; inversion compresses their net interest margins, leading them to tighten lending standards and reduce credit supply)
 Reduced credit availability then amplifies economic weakness, making recession more likely

  - Simple interest: interest earned on an investment is not reinvested
  - Compound interest: interest earned on an investment is reinvested (most common)
  - In finance we typically assume compound interest

## Slide 44  [layout: Blank]
 Present Value of a Lump Sum
 Discount future payments using current interest rates to find the present value (PV)
 PV  =  FVt[1/(1 + r)]t  =  FVt(PVIFr,t)
  - PV = present value of cash flow
  - FVt = future value of cash flow (lump sum) received in t period
  - r = annual interest rate
  - t = number of years in investment horizon
  - PVIFr,t = present value interest factor of a lump sum

## Slide 45  [layout: Blank]
 Future Value of a Lump Sum
 The future value (FV) of a lump sum received at the beginning of an investment horizon
 FVt  =  PV (1 + r)t  =  PV(FVIFr,t)
  - FVIFr,t = future value interest factor of a lump sum

## Slide 50  [layout: Blank]
 Key Takeaways
 Determinants of Interest Rates
  - Drivers: Real risk-free rate + Inflation expectations (Fisher Effect: i ≈ RFR + E(IP))
  - Market Equilibrium: Set by supply (savings) & demand (borrowing) for loanable funds.
  - Security-Specific: Yield = Base Rate + Premiums for Default, Liquidity, Maturity, & Special Features.
 Term Structure (Yield Curve)
  - Concept: Relationship between yield & maturity for similar-risk bonds.
  - Core Theories:
    - Expectations: Long-term rates = avg of expected future short rates.
    - Liquidity Premium: Investors demand extra compensation for longer maturities.
    - Market Segmentation: Supply and demand within specific maturity segments (e.g., short-term vs. long-term) set rates independently.
  - Key Insight: Inverted curve signals expected rate cuts & predicts recession.
