## Slide 5  [layout: Title Only]
 Multiple Roles of Banks
 Lender: Banks originate and hold loans (e.g., mortgages, business loans, consumer credit, syndicated facilities)
 Borrower: Banks borrow funds themselves — from depositors, other banks (interbank market), central banks (discount window), or wholesale markets (e.g., repos, commercial paper, bonds)
 Liquidity providers: lines of credit that firm can withdraw when needed
 Credit enhancement: provide letter of credit to firms guaranteeing that a buyer's payment to a seller will be received on time and for the correct amount
 Payment channel: Banks operate as the backbone of payment systems — processing checks, wires (e.g., SWIFT, Fedwire), cards

## Slide 12  [layout: Blank]
 Commercial Bank Liabilities
 Liabilities include:
  - Interest bearing deposits (savings account)
  - Non-interest bearing deposits (current account deposits)
  - Time deposits (fixed deposits)
  - Other borrowings by the bank (Fed funds, interbank borrowing, repos, etc.)
 Liabilities usually have shorter maturities and are more liquid than typical bank assets
  - Banks face maturity mismatch and liquidity risk
  - If short term rates go up suddenly, their cost of funds would go up but their interest income from assets would remain the same

## Slide 13  [layout: Blank]
 Commercial Bank Equity
 Equity
  - Surplus or additional paid-in capital
  - Common and preferred stocks
  - Retained earnings
 Minimum levels of equity capital are required by regulators to act as a buffer against losses
 If the cost of deposit funding is low, banks will likely keep equity capital levels to the minimum
  - This allows banks to put their funds to maximum use to earn profits.
  - The downside is that banks will be exposed to risk of insolvency if market conditions worsen suddenly.

## Slide 14  [layout: Blank]
 Off-Balance Sheet Activities
 Off-balance sheet (OBS) activities refer to financial arrangements, commitments, or exposures undertaken by commercial banks that do not appear as assets or liabilities on the bank's balance sheet under standard accounting rules
 OBS generate fee income, provide services to clients, manage risk, or create contingent obligations without immediately using the bank's balance sheet capacity
 Major categories of OBS items:
  - Unused loan commitments / lines of credit: Promises to lend money in the future (e.g., revolving credit facilities).
  - Letters of credit: Bank charges fee for guaranteeing future payment of a firm. If firm defaults, then bank adds item to liabilities.
  - Loans sold (securitization): If a bank sells off its loans, the bank may have to buy back the loan when its quality deteriorates (clawback provisions in the subprime mortgage crisis).
  - Derivative contracts: Buying or selling derivatives. When speculative bets turn out to be wrong, bank could face insolvency.
  [NOTES: The bank's letter of credit guarantees payment to the seller as long as agreed-upon delivery conditions have been met; i.e., if the good or service is provided and the buyer is unable to pay as agreed, the bank will make the payment on behalf of the buyer.]

## Slide 23  [layout: Blank]
 Bank Performance Measures
 Interest rate spread is the difference between lending and deposit rates
 Net interest margin is interest income minus interest expense divided by earning assets
 Net non-interest margin is non-interest income minus non-interest expense divided by earning assets
  - Trading profits, service charges on deposit accounts, fees from credit cards
 Net Charge-Offs is percentage of loans written-off as uncollectable
 Nonperforming loan (NPL) is the sum of borrowed money upon which the debtor has not made his scheduled payments for at least 90 day
 Return on assets (ROA) is net income divided by assets
 Return on equity (ROE) is net income divided by equity
 Bank profitability declines and charge-offs rise during economic recessions

## Slide 29  [layout: Blank]
 What are the major risks banks face?
 Commercial banks face a wide array of risks that can threaten their solvency, profitability, reputation, and operational continuity.
 1. Credit Risk
  - The risk of borrowers defaulting on loans or other credit exposures, leading to losses.
 2. Market Risk & Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book
  - Losses from adverse movements in interest rates, equity prices, FX, or commodities affecting trading books or asset-liability mismatches.
 3. Liquidity Risk
  - Inability to meet obligations without incurring unacceptable losses (funding or market liquidity).
 4. Operational Risk (Including Cyber & IT)
  - Losses from inadequate/failing internal processes, people, systems, or external events.
 5. Regulatory & Compliance Risk
  - Failure to meet laws, rules, or standards, leading to fines, restrictions, or forced changes.

## Slide 31  [layout: Blank]
 International Banking Regulations – Basel III
 Basel III is a comprehensive international regulatory framework for banks, developed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) aimed to strengthen the resilience of the global banking system by:
  - making banks hold more and better capital
  - reduce excessive leverage
  - improve liquidity management
  - enhance risk coverage
 Core Objectives and 3 Key Pillars
 Pillar 1: Minimum Capital Requirements
  - Higher quantity and quality of capital — emphasizes Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) as the highest-quality form (e.g., common shares and retained earnings).
  - Minimum CET1 ratio raised to 4.5% of risk-weighted assets (RWAs)

## Slide 32  [layout: Blank]
 International Banking Regulations – Basel III
 Pillar 2: Supervisory Review Process
  - Requires banks to assess their overall risk profile and supervisors to review it (SREP — Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process).
  - Promotes stronger governance, stress testing, and risk management.
 Pillar 3: Market Discipline
  - Mandates detailed, standardized public disclosures on capital, risk exposures, risk-weighted assets, leverage, liquidity, and remuneration.
  - Aims to enhance transparency so markets can better assess bank soundness.
 The most significant overhaul of banking regulation since the 1930s, shifting from a primarily risk-model-driven approach to one emphasizing higher-quality capital, non-risk-based leverage limits, and robust liquidity

## Slide 34  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 Investment Banks (IBs)
 Investment banking encompasses several distinct but interrelated activities. At its core, it refers to the financial intermediation services that help corporations, governments, and institutional investors raise capital, execute strategic transactions, and manage financial risk.
 The principal business lines include:
 Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A) Advisory: Providing buy-side and sell-side counsel on corporate transactions, valuations, and deal structures.
 Equity Capital Markets (ECM): Underwriting and distributing initial public offerings (IPOs), follow-on offerings, and convertible securities.
 Debt Capital Markets (DCM): Underwriting investment-grade and high-yield bonds, leveraged loans, and syndicated credit facilities.
 Sales & Trading: Intermediating client transactions and managing proprietary positions in equities, fixed income, currencies, and commodities (FICC).
 Research: Providing equity and credit analysis to institutional investors.

 Investment banks are commonly classified into four tiers:
 Bulge Bracket Banks

## Slide 43  [layout: Blank]
 Deal Valuation Methodologies
 Investment bankers regularly employ three core valuation approaches — always used in combination (a "football field"):
  - Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): Projects free cash flows (FCF) and discounts them at WACC (weighted-average cost of capital).
  - Comparable Company Analysis ("Comps"): Applies trading multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E, P/S) from public peers to the target company. Adjustments for size, growth, and liquidity are critical.
  - Precedent Transaction Analysis: Uses acquisition premiums and multiples from prior M&A deals. Typically yields higher valuations than comps due to control premiums (historically averaging 25–35%).

## Slide 45  [layout: Blank]
 Best Efforts vs. Firm Commitment: Example
 An investment bank agrees to underwrite an issue of 20,000,000 shares of stock for Murray Construction Corp. on a firm commitment basis. The investment bank pays $15.50 per share to Murray Construction Corp. for the 20,000,000 shares of stock. It then sells those shares to the public for $16.35 per share. What is the profit to the investment bank?
 If the investment bank can only sell the shares for $14.75, how much money does Murray Construction Corp. receive? What is the profit to the investment bank?
 Answer:
 The profit to the investment bank is ($16.35 - $15.50) × 20,000,000 shares = $17,000,000.
 From the perspective of Murray Construction Corp., the $17,000,000 represents the commission that it must pay to issue the stock.

## Slide 46  [layout: Blank]
 Best Efforts vs. Firm Commitment: Example
 If the investment bank sells the stock for $14.75 per share, Murray Construction Corp. still receives $15.50 × 20,000,000 shares = $310,000,000. The profit to the investment bank is ($14.75 - $15.50) × 20,000,000 shares = - $15,000,000.
 From the perspective of the investment bank, the - $15,000,000 represents the risk for the firm commitment it made to Murray Construction Corp. to issue the stock.
 Suppose instead the investment bank agrees to underwrite these 20,000,000 shares on a best efforts basis. The investment bank is able to sell 18,400,000 shares for $15.50 per share to public, and it charges Murray Construction Corp. $0.375 per share sold. How much money does Murray Construction Corp. receive? What is the profit to the investment bank?
  - If the investment bank sells the stock for $15.50 per share, Murray Construction Corp. receives ($15.50 - $0.375) × 18,400,000 shares = $278,300,000, the investment bank’s profit is $0.375 × 18,400,000 shares = $6,900,000

## Slide 59  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaway
 Core Function of Commercial Banks:
  - They act as intermediaries, taking deposits (liabilities) and issuing loans (assets).
  - They profit from the interest rate spread but face maturity mismatch and liquidity risk because their liabilities are typically short-term while assets are long-term.
 Bank Regulation:
  - Banks are heavily regulated to ensure stability and protect depositors.
  - Key regulators include the FDIC (deposit insurance), the Federal Reserve, and the OCC. Minimum equity capital is required to act as a buffer against losses.
 Investment Bank Activities:
  - Investment banks primarily operate in capital markets.
  - Their main roles include underwriting new securities (debt/equity) in the primary market and providing M&A advisory services.
  - They generate revenue through fees, commissions, and trading spreads.

## Slide 60  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaway
 Two ways to underwriting securities:
  - In a firm commitment, the investment bank bears the risk of unsold shares (buying the entire issue).
  - In a best efforts basis, the issuer bears the risk, and the bank only earns a commission on shares sold.
 Industry Structure & Evolution:
  - The Glass-Steagall Act (1933) originally separated commercial and investment banking.
  - This was effectively repealed in 1999, allowing for the creation of financial conglomerates (like BHCs and FHCs) that offer both services under one corporate umbrella.
