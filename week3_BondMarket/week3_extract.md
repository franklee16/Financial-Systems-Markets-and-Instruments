## Slide 3  [layout: Title and Content]
 Fixed-income Securities
 A fixed income security (a.k.a. debt security, bond) promises a fixed stream of income or a stream of income that is determined according to a pre-specified formula
 Key issues:
  - How to value future payments (time value of money)
  - Risk that promise will not be kept (credit risk)
 Two main types of payment streams:
  - Zero-coupon bonds make a single payment at the maturity (payment is face (or par) value)
  - Coupon bonds pay $C each period (typically every six months) up to maturity, when they pay $C + $face value
  [NOTES: Pre-specified formula allows for floating-rate bond. Promise is not necessarily fulfilled

One dollar return at different point in the future should be worth differently now

In this class we won’t deal with floating rate bonds]

## Slide 9  [layout: Blank]
 Treasury Notes and Bonds
 Treasury notes and bonds (T-notes and T-bonds) are issued by the U.S. Treasury to finance the national debt and other government expenditures
  - Default risk free: backed by “full faith and credit” of the U.S. federal government
  - Low returns: low interest rates reflect minimum default risk
  - Interest rate risk: because of their longer maturity, T-notes and T-bonds experience wider price fluctuations than money market securities (T-bills) when interest rates change
  - Liquidity risk: trade in very active secondary markets, but older T-bonds and T-notes have lower liquidity than newly issued T-bonds and T-notes
  - Inflation risk: Inflation may erode the purchasing power of nominal payments received in future
 T-notes have original maturities of 2, 3, 5, 7, and 10 years
 T-bonds have original maturities > 10 years
 Bond price is quoted as a percentage of the par value, which is usually $1,000
  [NOTES: Newly issued Treasuries are called ‘on the run’ and older Treasuries are called ‘off the run.’]

## Slide 15  [layout: Blank]
 Treasury Inflation Protected Securities
 Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) protect investors against inflation by adjusting the bond's principal daily based on changes in the Consumer Price Index (CPI-U).
 The coupon rate is fixed, but because it is applied to the inflation-adjusted principal, coupon payments rise with inflation.
  - Example: A TIPS with a $1,000 par value and a 3% coupon rate facing 10% annual inflation would see its principal adjust upward to $1,100.
  - The coupon payment would then be calculated as 3% of the adjusted principal ($1,100), resulting in $33 in annual interest payments rather than the $30 that would be paid on the unadjusted principal.
 TIPS provide a "real" yield—the return above inflation—while nominal Treasury securities provide a "nominal" yield that does not account for inflation's erosion of purchasing power.
 At maturity, investors receive the greater of the inflation-adjusted principal or the original face value — providing deflation protection.

## Slide 17  [layout: Blank]
 How the Treasury Market Works
 The US Treasury conducts regular auctions through the Treasury Automated Auction Processing System (TAAPS). Three types of bidders participate:
  - Primary Dealers: 24 designated counterparties (e.g., JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Citi) who are required to bid at all Treasury auctions at reasonably competitive prices. They serve as market-makers of last resort.
  - Direct Bidders: Primarily institutional investors (e.g., pension funds, central banks) accessing auctions directly through TAAPS.
  - Indirect Bidders: A heterogeneous category that includes foreign official institutions and investors routing through intermediaries — often interpreted as a proxy for foreign central bank demand.
 The secondary Treasury market is primarily an over-the-counter (OTC) dealer market, with primary dealers quoting bid-ask spreads. Daily Treasury trading volumes exceed $600 billion, making it the world's most liquid market.

## Slide 19  [layout: Blank]
 Fiscal Dynamics and the Bond Market
 The US government ran a fiscal deficit of approximately 6.4% of GDP in 2024
 These fiscal dynamics directly affect bond yields through two channels:
 Supply effect: Persistent large issuance requires the market to absorb more Treasury supply. If demand does not scale proportionally, yields must rise to attract buyers.
 Term premium channel: Fiscal uncertainty raises the risk premium investors demand for holding long-duration government bonds — the term premium — increasing long-end yields independently of policy rate expectations.

## Slide 21  [layout: Blank]
 Municipal Bond Yields
 Because municipal bonds are exempt from taxes, when comparing Munis with fully taxable corporate bonds, you need to find the after-tax yield for corporate bonds: ia = ib(1 – t)
    - ia = after-tax yield on corporate bond
    - ib = before-tax yield on corporate bond
    - t = marginal income tax rate of the bondholder
 Alternatively, convert the yield of municipal bonds to equivalent before-tax yield and compare with corporate bond yields:
 ib = ia/(1 – t)

## Slide 25  [layout: Blank]
 Convertible Bonds
 Corporate bonds often come with options attached (convertible and callable bonds)
 Convertible bonds give bondholders an option to exchange each bond for a specified number of common stocks of the issuing firm
  - For example, a convertible bond is issued at par value of $1,000. Bondholders have rights to convert each bond into 40 shares of the firm’s common stock (which currently trades at $20)
  - If converted now, the value is 40*$20=$800, which is less than the bond’s par value. So it is not profitable to convert now
  - However, when the stock prices rise above $25, conversion becomes profitable
 The convertible option is valuable because investors can benefit from a rise in the stock price of the issuing firm
  - Convertible bonds offer lower yields than comparable non-convertible bonds

## Slide 26  [layout: Blank]
 Callable Bonds
 Callable bonds are bonds with call provision, allowing the issuer to repurchase bonds at a pre-specified (call) price after a certain time
  - Bonds are usually called in when interest rates drop so that the issuer can refinance debt at lower interest rate
  - Bondholders dislike call provision because they have to re-invest at lower interest rate
  - To compensate investors for this cost, callable bonds offer higher yields than comparable non-callable bonds

## Slide 42  [layout: Title and Content]
 What Factors Affect Bond Yields?
 Default risk
  - Most important factor affecting bond yields
  - Investors require higher yields to hold bonds with higher default risk
 Maturity
  - Longer maturity bonds are more sensitive to interest rate changes, so typically have higher yields
 Liquidity
  - Less liquid bonds usually have higher yields to compensate investors for the higher trading costs
 Convertibility
  - Convertible bond has lower yields because investors get the valuable option to convert to (higher-value) stocks
 Callability
  - Callable bond enables the issuer to redeem the bonds before maturity at a pre-specified price. Because calls are typically exercised when interest rates fall, investors demand higher yields on callable bonds

## Slide 47  [layout: Title and Content]
 Interest Rate Risk
 Long-term bond prices are more sensitive to interest rate changes than short-term bonds
  - Intuitively, since the coupon rate is locked upfront, when interest rate changes, investors are locked in a better/worse deal for a longer time
 But not all same-maturity bonds are affected to the same degree
  - Bonds with higher coupon rate pay more of their cash flow early in the life, which can be reinvested at higher interest rate
  - So these bonds should be less sensitive to interest rate changes
 We would like a measure of how “long-term” a bond is, which captures the impacts of both maturity and coupon rates
  [NOTES: For higher-coupon bonds, you can get back a greater fraction of bond value in the interim and reinvest at prevailing interest rate]

## Slide 48  [layout: Blank]
 Duration
 Duration is a measure of the average maturity of a bond’s promised cash flows
 Duration equals the weighted average of the times to each coupon or principal payment
  - The weight applied to each payment time is the proportion of bond value accounted for by that payment (i.e., the present value of that payment divided by bond price)

## Slide 54  [layout: Blank]
 What Factors Determine Bond Duration?
 Duration and coupon rate
  - the higher the coupon rate, the shorter the bond’s duration
  - the higher the coupon rate, the earlier investors receive cash flows on a bond and the higher are the weights of those earlier cash flows in the duration calculation
 Duration and maturity
  - the longer the maturity, the longer the bond’s duration
  - duration generalizes the idea that long-term bonds are more sensitive to interest rate changes than short-term bonds
 Duration and yield to maturity
  - the higher the yield to maturity, the shorter the bond’s duration
  - higher YTM discount later cash flows more heavily, so the weights of those later payments are smaller in the duration calculation

## Slide 59  [layout: Blank]
 Convexity
 The bond price-interest rate relationship is non-linear, which is captured by convexity.
 Convexity (CX) measures the change in slope of the price-yield curve around interest rate level R
 Convexity incorporates the curvature of the price-yield curve into the estimated percentage price change of a bond given an interest rate change:
 Note the convexity term means that bond price will always be above the level predicted by duration model
 Convexity is a desirable property: all else equal, more-convex bonds outperform less-convex bonds when yields move significantly in either direction

## Slide 60  [layout: Blank]
 Key Takeaways
 Bond Basics: Fixed-income securities promising a stream of cash flows. Key types include Treasury (default-free), Municipal (tax-exempt), and Corporate (subject to default risk).
 Bond Features:
  - TIPS: Principal adjusts with inflation, providing a real yield.
  - Convertible: Holder can exchange for stock; offers lower yields.
  - Callable: Issuer can redeem early; offers higher yields.
 Bond Pricing & Yields:
  - Price = PV of future coupons + PV of par (using semi-annual discounting).
  - Core Principle: Bond prices have an inverse relationship with interest rates (yields).
 Interest Rate Risk:
  - Long-term & low-coupon bonds are most sensitive to rate changes.
  - Duration: Measures a bond's average maturity & price sensitivity.
