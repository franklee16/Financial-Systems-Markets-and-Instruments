## Slide 4  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 The Money Markets Defined
 The term “money market” is a misnomer. Money (currency) is not actually traded in the money markets.
 The securities in the money market are short term with high liquidity and low risk; therefore, they are close to being money.
 Money markets serve three core economic functions:
  - Cash management: governments, corporations, and financial institutions fund day-to-day liquidity needs
  - Monetary policy transmission: the Federal Reserve uses money market rates as its primary policy lever
  - Safe asset provision: investors seeking capital preservation park funds in near-zero-default-risk instruments

## Slide 5  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 Key Characteristics of the Money Market
 Short maturity: Typically very short-term — from overnight to one year (often 1–3 months or 90 days)
 Low risk: Instruments are high-quality with minimal credit and interest rate risk (often backed by governments, top-rated banks, or strong corporations)
 High liquidity: Assets can usually be sold or redeemed quickly with little price impact
 Purpose: Focuses on cash management rather than long-term investment or capital appreciation — returns are modest compared to stocks or bonds

## Slide 7  [layout: Blank]
 Participant Ecosystem
 The money market operates across a layered ecosystem:
  - Cash suppliers (lenders): MMFs, corporations, GSEs, foreign central banks, and retail investors
  - Cash demanders (borrowers): Primary dealers, commercial banks, the US Treasury (T-bill issuance), and corporations issuing CP
  - Central intermediary: The Federal Reserve sets the policy rate corridor that anchors all short-term rates
  - Regulators: SEC governs MMFs under Rule 2a-7; Fed and OCC supervise banks; FINRA oversees dealer activity

## Slide 8  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 Why Do We Need Money Markets?
 The banking industry should handle the needs for short-term investment and financing.
 Banks have an information advantage.
 Banks, however, are heavily regulated.
 Creates a distinct cost advantage for money markets over banks.

## Slide 9  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 Money Market vs. Bank Deposits
 Reserve requirements create additional costs for banks that money markets do not bear.
 Regulation Q originated in the Banking Act of 1933 has two main components:
  - Prohibition on paying any interest on demand deposits (checking accounts).
  - Imposition of maximum interest rate ceilings on time and savings deposits at commercial banks (later extended to thrifts like savings & loans).
 Limits on interest rate banks could offer was not relevant until the 1950s. In the decades that followed, the problem became apparent.

## Slide 15  [layout: Blank]
 T-bill Auctions in Primary Market
 Maturities of 4-week, 8-week, 13-week, 17-week, 26-week, and 52-week are auctioned on varying weekly schedules
 Bids are submitted by government securities dealers, financial and nonfinancial corporations, and individuals
 Bids are submitted as either competitive (specifying the discount rate accepted) or noncompetitive (accepting the clearing rate)
    - All bidders pay the lowest accepted price (highest accepted yield – also called “stop out yield”)
    - Bidders at the “stop out yield” may receive a pro-rata share of their allotment.
 Most secondary trading occurs directly through brokers and dealers
  [NOTES: 2-, 3-, 5-, and 7-year notes are auctioned monthly

## Slide 19  [layout: 2_Blank]
 We use the formula below to calculate price of the T-bill:
 Price = 10,000*[1 - i * (n/ 360)]
 n = number of days until maturity
 i = prevailing yield
 Assuming par value = $10,000 and one year has 360 days
 Example
 Using T-bill maturing on 10 August 2023
 Ask Price for a $10,000 par value T-bill
 = 10,000*(1- 4.855%*153/360)=$9793.66
 Bid Price for a $10,000 par value T-bill

## Slide 20  [layout: Blank]
 Federal Funds
 Federal funds (or fed funds) = balances that banks hold in their reserve accounts at the Federal Reserve
 The federal funds market involves overnight, unsecured lending of reserve balances between depository institutions at the Federal Reserve
 Banks with excess reserves lend fed funds, while banks with deficient reserves borrow fed funds
  - Transactions are settled via Fedwire (the Federal Reserve's real-time gross settlement system for transferring reserve balances instantly)
 The Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR) is the volume-weighted median of these transactions, and the FOMC sets a target range for EFFR as its primary policy

## Slide 23  [layout: Blank]
 Federal Funds Transaction
 Today: The Loan Initiation
  - J.P. Morgan Chase has extra reserves it doesn't need right now.
  - Bank of America (BOA) needs more reserves (e.g., to meet regulatory requirements, cover outflows, or avoid overdrafts).
  - J.P. Morgan lends (or "sells") $75 million in federal funds to BOA.
  - In practice, this is an unsecured overnight loan — BOA promises to repay principal + interest tomorrow.
 Tomorrow: Repayment with Interest
  - The loan is overnight, so repayment happens the next business day.
  - BOA repays the $75 million principal plus one day's interest (calculated at the agreed federal funds rate for that transaction).
  - Via another Fedwire transaction (reverse direction):
    - FRB San Francisco debit $75m + interest from BOA's reserve account.
    - FRBNY credit $75m + interest to J.P. Morgan Chase's reserve account.
  - The lender (J.P. Morgan) earns the interest as income, while the borrower (BOA) pays it as the cost of borrowing reserves overnight.

## Slide 24  [layout: Blank]
 Repurchase Agreement
 A repurchase agreement (repo or RP) is the sale of a security with an agreement to buy the security back at a higher price in the future
 Repos are essentially short-term collateralized loans (typical collateral is U.S. Treasury securities)
  - The repo rate is typically lower than the Fed funds rate because repos are collateralized loans.
 If collateralized by risky assets, the repo may involve a ‘haircut’.
  - For instance, to borrow $100 the repo seller would have to sell securities currently worth $102, for a $2 haircut.
 A reverse repurchase agreement is the purchase of a security with an agreement to sell it back in the future

## Slide 25  [layout: Blank]
 Commercial Paper
 Commercial paper is a short-term unsecured debt instrument issued by corporations to fund working capital needs — typically accounts receivable, inventory financing, or bridge funding.
 Key features:
 Maturities of 1–270 days
 Ratings are essential — most CP is rated A-1/P-1 (prime) or A-2/P-2 by Moody's/S&P
 CP is usually held by investors until maturity and has no active secondary market
 Yields are quoted using discount yield (like T-bills)
 Total outstanding in early 2026: ~$1.43 trillion

## Slide 27  [layout: Blank]
 Negotiable Certificate of Deposits
 A negotiable certificate of deposit (NCD) is a bank-issued time deposit that specifies the interest rate and the maturity date
  - Denominations range from $100,000 to $10 million; $1 million being the most common
 Unlike standard retail CDs, NCDs have market-determined interest rates and can be liquidated before maturity by selling to another investor
 Often purchased by money market mutual funds with pools of funds from individual investors

## Slide 28  [layout: Blank]
 Banker’s Acceptance
 Banker's Acceptance (BA) is a short-term debt instrument commonly used in international trade finance
  - to facilitate secure payments between buyers (importers) and sellers (exporters)
  - especially when the parties do not fully trust each other or when extended payment terms are needed
 A time draft (a written order to pay a specified amount at a future date) that has been "accepted" by a bank
 By accepting the draft, the bank unconditionally promises to pay the face value of the draft on the maturity date
 Replaces the importer's credit risk with the bank's credit quality, which is usually much stronger and more reliable

## Slide 31  [layout: Blank]
 Why Banker’s Acceptance Are Attractive to Investors?
 Banker's acceptances trade at a discount in the secondary money market and are considered one of the safest short-term instruments
  - Often safer than commercial paper because of the bank's unconditional guarantee
  - They typically yield slightly more than Treasury bills but less than regular commercial paper due to their low risk
 In short: A banker's acceptance transforms a risky importer’s promise into a safe, bank-guaranteed, tradable short-term credit instrument — one of the classic tools that helped make global trade much safer and more efficient

## Slide 35  [layout: Blank]
 International Money Markets
 Eurodollars are US dollar-denominated deposits held outside the United States — in London, Singapore, Hong Kong, the Cayman Islands, and other offshore centers.
 The term "Euro" is historical and geographical; any offshore USD deposit qualifies.
  - Not subject to Federal Reserve reserve requirements or FDIC insurance — giving offshore banks both higher flexibility and higher credit risk
  - Historically, the rate offered for borrowing on Eurodollar funds is the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR)
  - Estimated outstanding volume: $13–15 trillion in deposits and loans, or $30–40 trillion including derivatives and repo exposure

## Slide 36  [layout: Blank]
 LIBOR-to-SOFR Transition
 One of the most consequential structural changes in money markets over the past decade was the transition from USD LIBOR to SOFR as the primary short-term dollar benchmark.
 LIBOR (London Interbank Offered Rate) was a panel-based, unsecured rate — an average of rates at which banks reported they could borrow. It was the global reference rate for ~$350 trillion in financial contracts.
 Its fatal flaw emerged in 2012: manipulation allegations showed that banks had submitted false quotes, rendering LIBOR untrustworthy as a benchmark.
 SOFR, administered by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, is a transaction-based, secured rate
  - a volume-weighted median of actual overnight repo transactions collateralized by US Treasury securities.
  [NOTES: Details of how the manipulation was accomplished to earn profits may be found in the Wall Street Journal Online article of 5/2/13: Clubby London Trading Scene Fostered Libor Rate-Fixing Scandal http://online.wsj.com/article/SB10001424127887323296504578396670651342096.html?mod=WSJ_hp_LEFTWhatsNewsCollection.
 
UBS was fined $1.52 billion, RBS $612 million and Barclays $450 million.  J. P. Morgan an]

  - Single payment yields (isp):  Interest rate is quoted on an annual basis assuming a 360-day year as a percentage of purchase price

## Slide 46  [layout: Title and Content]
 Money Market Funds (MMFs)
 MMFs are pooled investment vehicles that invest primarily in money market securities
  - such as U.S. Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, repurchase agreements (repos)
 They function as de facto bank accounts for institutional cash
 The primary objectives of MMFs are to:
  - Preserve capital
  - Provide daily liquidity
  - Provide competitive yields to investors
  - Maintain a stable net asset value (NAV) typically at $1 per share

## Slide 48  [layout: Title and Content]
 MMF Types and Structures
 Government MMFs: invest ≥99.5% in US government securities, repo collateralized by government securities, and cash. Must maintain stable $1.00 NAV for all investor class
 Prime MMFs: invest in any money market instrument including CP, CDs, and repo. Retail prime funds maintain stable NAV; institutional prime funds must use floating NAV
 Tax-Exempt/Municipal MMFs: invest in short-term municipal paper; income is federally tax-free

## Slide 52  [layout: Title and Content]
 Regulatory Architecture for MMFs
 SEC Rule 2a-7 (under the Investment Company Act of 1940) governs all US money market funds. Its key requirements:
  - Portfolio maturity limits (WAM ≤60 days; WAL ≤120 days)
  - Credit quality standards (only "eligible securities" rated A-1/P-1 or equivalent)
  - Diversification (no single issuer >5% except government securities)
  - Liquidity minimums (daily ≥25%; weekly ≥50% after 2023 reform)
  - Reporting (monthly Form N-MFP to SEC; publicly available)

## Slide 53  [layout: Title and Content]
 Money Market Fund Yields
 MMF yields respond almost mechanically to the federal funds rate because their portfolio maturities are so short (WAM typically 30–45 days) that most holdings reprice within weeks of any rate change.
 MMF Yield ≈ EFFR − Expense Ratio ± Portfolio Alpha
  - Government MMF yields closely track the Fed funds rate minus management fees (~10–15 bps).
  - Prime MMFs earn a spread above government MMFs (~10–30 bps) by accepting CP and CD credit exposure.

## Slide 56  [layout: Title and Content]
 MMFs: What are the Risks?
 MMFs aim for low volatility and are considered among the safest investments, though they are not entirely risk-free:
  - Interest Rate Risk: Yields fall with declining rates, potentially underperforming inflation.
  - Credit Risk: Minimal but possible if issuers default.
  - Liquidity Risk: In market stress, funds may face redemption pressures, leading to forced sales at losses.
  - No Government Insurance: Unlike bank deposits, no FDIC protection; SIPC covers only brokerage failures.
  - Opportunity Cost: Lower returns than stocks or longer-term bonds.

## Slide 57  [layout: Title and Content]
 Mini Case: The 2008 GFC and Reserve Primary Fund
 The 2008 financial crisis highlighted vulnerabilities when the Reserve Primary Fund broke the buck due to Lehman Brothers exposure
 Following the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers on September 15, the Reserve Primary Fund faced a massive wave of redemption requests (a "run").
 On September 16, the fund wrote down its Lehman Brothers holdings to zero
 Consequently, the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV) fell from the stable $1.00 standard to $0.97.
 This was only the second time in history a money market fund had "broken the buck," shattering investor confidence in what was considered a risk-free asset

## Slide 59  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 What is Money Market?
  - A market for short-term (≤ 1 year), highly liquid, and low-risk securities.
  - Purpose: Manage short-term funding needs & invest excess cash safely (not long-term growth).
 Key Money Market Instruments:
  - Treasury Bills (T-bills): Short-term gov't debt; default-risk free; sold at a discount.
  - Federal Funds: Overnight, unsecured loans between banks (Fed Funds Rate = policy target).
  - Repos (RP): Short-term, collateralized loans (using Treasuries); rate < Fed Funds.
  - Commercial Paper (CP): Unsecured, short-term corporate debt; limited secondary market.
  - Negotiable CDs: Bank-issued time deposits; bearer instruments (tradable).
  - Banker's Acceptances (BA): Bank-guaranteed trade finance instrument; low risk.
