## Slide 10  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why do We Care About Market Efficiency?
 Deviations from efficiency may offer profitable opportunities to better-informed traders at the expense of less-informed traders
 Inefficient markets  mispriced securities  suboptimal resource allocation
  - Overvalued firms can raise capital too cheaply and overinvest
  - Undervalued firms may have to pass up profitable opportunities because cost of capital is too high
 Thus, efficient security prices help to allocate resources efficiently

## Slide 12  [layout: Title and Content]
 Weak Form Market Efficiency
 Stock prices already reflect all information contained in the history of past prices and trading volumes
 Reasoning: If useful, investors have learned to trade on this information thus impounding it into prices, removing further profit opportunities
 Technical Analysis - using historical prices and volume information to predict future price changes
 Weak-form efficiency  Technical analysis should not work

## Slide 13  [layout: Title and Content]
 Semi-Strong Form Market Efficiency
 Prices reflect all publicly available information
    - Historical price/volume plus all public information on firm fundamentals, including revenues and costs, balance sheet strength, patents held, quality of management etc.
 Reasoning: If useful, investors have learned to trade on this information thus already impounding it into prices, removing further profit opportunities
 Fundamental Analysis - attempts to identify mispriced securities by examining relevant economic/financial factors including the balance sheet, consumer demand, investments
 Semi-strong form efficiency  fundamental analysis should not work

 Evidence Against Market Efficiency
 It is not surprising that market is not strong-form efficient
  - The ability of insiders to trade profitably in stock market is well documented by academic studies
  - SEC closely monitors and prosecutes insider trading cases
 In a well-functioning market, we should at least expect market to be semi-strong form efficient
 Recent studies document evidence that the stock market is not even weak-form efficient
  - Both historical prices/volume and current firm fundamental information can predict future stock movement

## Slide 25  [layout: Title and Content]
 Mini Case Study: The Dot-Com Bubble (1995-2000) — Irrational Exuberance or Rational Revolution?
 The Technological Catalyst
 The Internet Revolution: The commercialization of the World Wide Web (early 1990s) promised to fundamentally reshape commerce, communication, and information.
 Key Metrics That Defied Tradition: Companies were valued on "eyeballs" (web traffic), user growth, and platform potential rather than earnings, cash flows, or assets.
 The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) Viewpoint: The market was rationally pricing the option value of market dominance in a winner-take-most digital economy. High uncertainty justified high valuations.
 In 1998, was it rational to value Amazon (which had never shown a profit) higher than established retailers like Barnes & Noble? Why or why not?

## Slide 26  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Bubble Inflation Mechanism (1998-2000)
 Venture Capital Fuel: Easy money flowed into startups with ".com" in their name.
 IPO Frenzy: Companies with minimal revenue went public and saw first-day pops of 100%+ (e.g., VA Linux, up 698% on debut).
 Retail Mania: Online brokerages (E*Trade) and day trading became mainstream.
 Media & Analyst Hype: Traditional valuation metrics were dismissed as "old economy" thinking. "This time is different" became the mantra.
 Momentum Investing: Rising prices attracted more buyers in a self-reinforcing cycle, decoupling prices from any plausible fundamentals.

## Slide 27  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Prick & Collapse (2000-2002)
 March 10, 2000: NASDAQ Composite peaks at 5,048.62 (up from ~750 in 1995).
 Catalysts:
  - Interest Rate Hikes: The Fed raised rates to cool the economy.
  - Profit Warnings: Internet infrastructure companies (e.g., Cisco) signalled slowing demand.
  - Lock-Up Expirations: Insiders could finally sell their shares, flooding the market.
  - Scepticism Grows: Barron's "Burning Up" cover story (March 2000) detailed cash burn rates.
 The Aftermath
  - NASDAQ fell ~78% by October 2002.
  - Most dot-com companies failed; survivors (Amazon, eBay) saw 90%+ drawdowns.
  - ~$5 trillion in market value evaporated.

## Slide 28  [layout: Title and Content]
 A Failure of Market Efficiency?
 Investor Irrationality: Overconfidence, herd behavior, and extrapolation bias.
 Agency Problems: Analysts at investment banks faced conflicts (underwriting relationships) and issued overly optimistic reports.
 Asymmetric Information: Insiders (VCs, founders) understood the fragility but had incentives to promote the narrative.
 "Irrational exuberance" — Alan Greenspan (1996), warning years before the peak
 But…
 "The Dot-Com Bubble proves markets are inefficient because prices clearly deviated from intrinsic value. However, it also proved efficient in the long run by rapidly allocating massive capital to a transformative technology, allowing the optimal business models to emerge through a Darwinian process. Which perspective is more compelling, and what does that tell us about the time horizon of market efficiency?"

## Slide 35  [layout: Title and Content]
 Value Premium: Competing Explanations
 The risk-based view holds that value firms have higher leverage, more irreversible capital, and greater exposure to financial distress risk during downturns — they are inherently riskier.
 The behavioral view, articulated by Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1994), holds that investors extrapolate past growth too far, overpricing glamour stocks and underpricing value stocks.
 Both explanations found some support in data.

## Slide 39  [layout: Title and Content]
 Low Volatility Anomaly: Explanation
 The most plausible explanation for the low volatility anomaly is investors’ preference for lottery type of stocks
  - Investors are willing to accept a bet that has a small chance of winning big, but on average earns a lower return
  - For example, people typically prefer to have a 1/1000 chance of winning $5,000 than to have $10 with certainty (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992)
  - High-volatility stocks resemble a lottery, and are thus overvalued by investors
 Evidence: Kumar (2009) show that retail investors who spend more money on lotteries also buy more high-volatility stocks
  [NOTES: Note that risk averse investors would reject the fair game]

## Slide 48  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Paradox of Efficiency
 The Paradox of Efficiency
  - If everyone believes markets are efficient, no one will bother to seek information, making price inefficient.
  - The very act of trying to beat the market is what makes it efficient. (Grossman-Stiglitz Paradox).
 The EMH as a Lens
  - It's the default, null hypothesis. The burden of proof is on the active manager.
  - It is remarkably robust but not a perfect description of reality.
  - Finance has evolved from "Are markets efficient?" to "How efficient, under what conditions, and where are the persistent inefficiencies?"

## Slide 50  [layout: Title and Content]
 Smart Beta ETFs
 All these anomalies sound great, but can investors benefit from them?
 In the past, no easy way to benefit from these anomalies
 Only high net worth investors who can invest in hedge funds that invest systematically in these strategies
 Recently, many Smart Beta ETFs launched by asset management firms allow retail investors to easily invest in such strategies
  - An exchange-traded fund that tracks a non-market-cap-weighted index, constructed according to transparent, rules-based criteria that tilt portfolio exposures toward one or more investment factors
  - Allow investors to invest in some of the proven strategies used by hedge funds at much lower cost and with great transparency

## Slide 51  [layout: Title and Content]
 Smart Beta ETFs: Passive or Active?
 Smart beta ETFs blur the line between passive and active investing
  - Passive because it is rules-based, transparent, and low cost relative to active funds
  - Active because it makes deliberate active choices about index design — which factors to target, how to measure them, how to weight securities, and how frequently to rebalance
  [TABLE]
    | Strategy | Approach | Avg Expense Ratio | Factor Transparency |
    | Market-cap ETF | Full passive replication | ~0.10% | None |
    | Smart Beta ETF | Rules-based factor tilt | ~0.33% | High |
    | Active Quant Fund | Model-driven with PM discretion | ~0.60% | Moderate |
    | Traditional Active | Stock-picker judgment | ~1.00% | Low |

## Slide 52  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Academic Genesis — Factor Investing
 The intellectual lineage of smart beta begins with the Capital Asset Pricing Model which posits that only one systematic risk factor — market beta — prices securities in equilibrium.
 The factor investing revolution began when Fama and French (1993) demonstrated that two additional factors beyond market beta — a size premium (SMB: small minus big) and a value premium (HML: high minus low book-to-market) — explain a large portion of the cross-section of equity returns that CAPM cannot.
 Carhart (1997) added a momentum factor (MOM: past-12-month winners minus losers, skipping one month) to produce the canonical four-factor model.
 Fama and French (2015) further extended their framework with two factors related to investment and profitability
  [IMAGE -> figures/slide52_img.png]

## Slide 53  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Six Core Investable Factors
 Despite the proliferation of proposed factors, practitioner consensus — reflected in products from BlackRock iShares, MSCI, and S&P DJI — has converged on a manageable set of empirically and theoretically motivated factors
  [TABLE]
    | Factor | Definition | Measurement | Economic Rationale |
    | Value | Cheap stocks vs. expensive | P/B, P/E, P/S ratios | Risk compensation or mean-reversion of mispricing |
    | Size | Small-cap vs. large-cap | Market capitalization | Liquidity/information risk premium |
    | Momentum | Past winners vs. losers | 12-1 month returns | Underreaction, behavioral extrapolation |
    | Quality | High profitability, stable earnings | ROE, accruals, leverage | Risk of distress; earnings persistence |
    | Low Volatility | Low-risk stocks | Historical return variance | Lottery demand, leverage constraints |
    | Investment | Conservative vs. aggressive capex | Asset growth, capex | Asset pricing / q-theory |

## Slide 55  [layout: Title and Content]
 Factor Cyclicality
 A critical practitioner insight is that factor premia are not constant — they vary systematically with the macroeconomic and credit cycle:
  - During expansions (rising growth): Value and Momentum perform best; Low Volatility lags.
  - During contractions (falling growth): Quality and Low Volatility provide defensive outperformance; Value and Momentum suffer sharply.
  - During recoveries: Size (equal weight) and Value tend to outperform as risk appetite returns.

## Slide 61  [layout: Title and Content]
 Portfolio Construction Applications
 Core-Satellite Framework
  - Smart beta ETFs are most commonly deployed in a core-satellite architecture
  - a passive market-cap core (60–80% of equity allocation) provides low-cost broad market exposure
  - while smart beta satellites (20–40%) target factor exposures designed to tilt the overall portfolio toward desired outcomes — higher expected return, lower volatility, or income generation

## Slide 62  [layout: Title and Content]
 Portfolio Construction Applications
 Strategic Factor Allocation
  - Long-horizon investors (pension funds, endowments): Prioritize value, size, and profitability — factors with the longest academic documentation and the most compelling risk-based rationale.
  - Volatility-sensitive investors: Minimum volatility and quality factors reduce downside capture while maintaining moderate upside participation.
  - Income-focused investors: High dividend yield ETFs (a form of smart beta) provide both factor exposure (value tilt) and current income.
 Factor Timing — Mostly Unadvisable
  - Despite the documented cyclicality of factor premia, the empirical literature on factor timing is discouraging.
  - The theoretical justification — using relative valuations of factor portfolios to rotate between factors — has not produced consistently strong out-of-sample results.
  - The consensus recommendation is to maintain diversified, rebalanced multi-factor exposures rather than attempting factor timing.

## Slide 63  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH): Security prices fully reflect all available information. New information is random → prices follow a random walk & are unpredictable.
 Three Forms of EMH:
  - Weak-Form: Prices reflect past prices/volume → Technical Analysis useless.
  - Semi-Strong: Prices reflect all public info → Fundamental Analysis useless.
  - Strong: Prices reflect all info → No one can beat market (including insider).
 Evidence Against Efficiency (Anomalies):
  - Momentum: Past winners continue to win (violates weak-form).
  - Post-Earnings Drift: Prices react slowly to earnings news (violates semi-strong).
  - Asset Bubbles: Prices deviate dramatically from fundamentals (Dot-Com bubble).
  - Anomalies: Size, Value, Low Volatility, Profitability, Investment effects generate persistent risk-adjusted returns (alpha).

## Slide 64  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 Interpreting Anomalies:
  - Risk-Based: Anomalies are compensation for hidden risks.
  - Behavioral: Result of investor biases (overreaction, lottery preference).
  - Data Mining: Some patterns may be found by chance (not replicable).
