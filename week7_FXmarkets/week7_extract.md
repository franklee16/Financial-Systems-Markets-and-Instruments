## Slide 3  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Foreign Exchange Market
 The Foreign Exchange Market can be defined in terms of specific functions, or the institutional structure that:
 (1)  Facilitates the conversion of one country’s currency into another.
  - Through the buying and selling of currencies.
  - Allows global firms to move in and out of foreign currency as needed.
  - (2) Sets and quotes exchange rates.
  - This is the ratio of one currency to another.
  - These rates determine costs and returns to global businesses.
 (3) Offers contracts to manage foreign exchange exposure.
  - These hedging contracts allow global firms to offset their foreign currency exposures and manage foreign exchange risk.
  - Thus, they can concentrate on their core business.

## Slide 4  [layout: Blank]
 Spot and Forward Transaction
 Foreign exchange (FX) markets are markets in which one currency is exchanged for another, either today or at a set time in the future:
  - Spot transactions involve the immediate exchange of currencies at current exchange rates (account for approximately 27% of global FX turnover)
  - Forward transactions involve the exchange of currencies at a specified exchange rate at a specific date in the future (19% of global turnover)
  - FX swaps combine a spot leg and a forward leg—the two most commonly identical in notional amount but opposite in direction (42% of global turnover)

## Slide 6  [layout: Title and Content]
 Market Participants
 Large global banks (e.g., Deutsche Bank, HSBC, Citibank) acting on behalf of:
  - (1) Their “external” clients” (primarily global firms: exporters, importers, multinational firms)
    - Acting in a broker capacity at the request of these clients and meeting the foreign currency needs of these clients.
  - (2) Their own banks (trading to generate profits)
    - Acting in a “dealer” (i.e., trading) capacity
    - Taking positions in currencies to make a profit
 In meeting the needs of their clients and their own trading activities, these global banks “establish” the “tone” of the market
  - This is through a “market maker” function

## Slide 7  [layout: Blank]
 Foreign Exchange Risk
 Risk that the value of the foreign currency may change relative to the domestic currency
 Currency depreciation occurs when a country’s currency falls in value relative to other currencies:
  - domestic goods become cheaper for foreign buyers
  - foreign goods become more expensive for domestic consumers
 Currency appreciation occurs when a country’s currency rises in value relative to other currencies
 Foreign exchange risk can come from holding foreign assets and/or liabilities that are mismatched

## Slide 17  [layout: Title and Content]
 Dollar Dominance
 The US dollar is involved in approximately 88% of all FX transactions (on one side)—a figure largely unchanged for over two decades.
 This dominance reflects deep network effects: because dollar-denominated contracts are universal, markets are maximally liquid, pricing is standardized, and switching costs are enormous.
 When Thailand buys Brazilian cotton, the transaction typically goes through USD even though neither party is American.
 The dollar's reserve currency share has declined from over 70% in 2000 to a record-low 56.3% of allocated global reserves in mid-2025.

## Slide 18  [layout: Title and Content]
 Renminbi Internationalization
 The declining share reflects deliberate diversification by central banks amid geopolitical risk, US sanctions policy, and US policy unpredictability.
  - The euro holds ~20%, the yen ~6%, the pound ~5%, and the renminbi ~2%
 China has actively promoted RMB internationalization since 2009
  - cross-border trade settlement, bilateral currency swap agreements with 30+ countries, establishment of the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), and inclusion in the IMF SDR basket (2016).
 Still remains far behind the USD, EUR and JPY
 The fundamental constraint is capital controls
  - without free convertibility and deep, liquid domestic capital markets accessible to foreign investors, the RMB cannot function as a genuine international reserve asset.

## Slide 22  [layout: Title and Content]
 Bid-Ask Spread
 Usually no explicit FX commissions, but there are bid-ask spreads.
  - E.g. GBP per USD: 0.8395 (bid), 0.8396 (ask)
 Foreign exchange dealers buy currency at the bid price and sell currency at the ask price. Hence the bid and ask are from the dealer’s perspective.
  - Bid: Dealer will buy the reference currency at this price if you are selling
  - Ask: Dealer is asking this price if you are buying the reference currency.
 Example:
 0.8395 is a bid price for USD. The dealer will buy USD (reference currency) at GBP 0.8395.
 0.8396 is a ask price for USD. The dealer will sell USD (reference currency) at GBP 0.8396.

## Slide 27  [layout: Blank]
 The Importance of Hedging Foreign Exchange Risk
 Internationally active firms often seek to hedge their foreign currency exposure
  - By using derivatives such as swaps and futures
  - By borrowing money in the same currency (and incurring local currency interest expenses) in which they are earning revenues
  - By locating production facilities (and incurring local currency operating costs) where they are earning revenues

## Slide 28  [layout: Blank]
 Foreign Exchange Risk: Example
 A US FI has the following assets and liabilities:
 Assets: $100 million US 1-yr loan in USD with 9% interest rate and £ 62.5 million UK 1-yr loan in GBP with 15% interest rate
 Liabilities: $200 million 1-yr USD Deposits with 8% interest rate
 Spot exchange rate is 1.6$/ £ 1, so £ 62.5 million=$100 million
 If spot rate at end of year doesn’t change, the dollar proceeds from UK loan is £62.5*(1+15%)*$1.6/ £ 1=$115-million-or-15%-dollar return
 Net return is 0.5*9%+0.5*15%-8%=4%
 If GBP depreciated in value against USD from $1.6/ £ 1 to $1.45/ £ 1 at the end of year, the dollar value of UK loan will be:
 £62.5*(1+15%)* $1.45/ £ = $104.22 million-or-4.22%-dollar return
 Net return becomes negative: 0.5*9%+0.5*4.22%-8% = -1.39%

## Slide 29  [layout: Blank]
 Hedging Foreign Exchange Risk
 Firms can hedge their foreign exchange exposure either on or off the balance sheet
 On-balance-sheet hedging involves matching foreign assets and liabilities
  - as foreign exchange rates move, any decreases in foreign asset values are offset by decreases in foreign liability values (and vice versa)
 Off-balance-sheet hedging involves the use of forward contracts
  - forward contracts are entered into (at t = 0) that specify exchange rates to be used in the future (i.e., no matter what the prevailing spot exchange rates are at t = 1)

## Slide 30  [layout: Blank]
 Hedging on the Balance Sheet: Example
 Using the same example, FI can fund the £ 62.5 million UK loan with equivalent 1-yr pound deposits at 11% interest rate.
 If spot rate doesn’t change at end of year, Net return is
 (0.5*9%+0.5*15%)-(0.5*8%+0.5*11%)=2.5%
 When GBP depreciates against USD from $1.6/ £ 1 to $1.45/ £, the dollar return on UK loan is 4.22%.
 The dollar value of UK liability is:
 £ 62.5*(1+11%)* $1.45/ £ = $100.59 million, or 0.59% funding cost.
 Average return on assets is 0.5*9%+0.5*4.22%=6.61%
 Average funding cost is 0.5*8%+0.5*0.59%=4.295%
 Net return= Average return on assets - Average funding cost=2.315%, similar to no exchange rate movement case.

## Slide 31  [layout: Blank]
 Hedging off the Balance Sheet: Example
 Instead of hedging with currency matched assets and liabilities, FI could hedge currency risk using forward as a low-cost alternative.
 Using the same example, FI sells the expected principal and interest income from UK loan forward at current forward rate for 1-yr delivery.
 Assume current forward rate with 1-yr delivery is 1.55$/ £1
 At end of year, FI deliver £ 62.5*(1+15%) million to the buyer of the forward contract and receives £ 62.5*(1+15%)*1.55$/ £1=$111.406 million, or a guaranteed dollar return of 11.406%
 Net return is thus 0.5*9%+0.5*11.406%-8%=2.203%

## Slide 33  [layout: Title and Content]
 How do exchange rates move?
 Some theories describe how exchange rates should move. We will examine in detail:
  - Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)
  - Relative PPP
  - Interest Rate Parity (IRP)
 Most parity conditions rely on the law of one price, which must hold in a no-arbitrage equilibrium.
  - Arbitrage is basically the simultaneous buying and selling of assets for the purpose of making a risk-free profit.
 But how do exchange rates actually move?
  - Does uncovered interest rate parity hold? If not, we can do the carry trade to profit from it.

## Slide 38  [layout: Title and Content]
 Interest Rate Parity (IRP)
 Interest rate parity (IRP) is an arbitrage condition that holds when international currency markets and money markets are in equilibrium.
 IRP states that the following two investment strategies should yield the same rate of return:
  - Invest $1 in the US (in a risk-free deposit) at r$ p.a. for one year.
  - Convert $1 into FC and invest the FC in a risk-free deposit at rFC p.a. for one year. Enter into a forward contract now to sell the FC at the one-year forward rate to convert the FC into USD.
 Both strategies are riskless and as a result, they should yield the same rate of return.
 Forward contracts allow us to completely hedge the risk of future currency movements.

## Slide 39  [layout: Title and Content]
 What are Forward Contracts?
 A forward contract is a way to lock-in a rate to transact a foreign currency in the future.
 Contrast with spot contracts which occur now. If you buy or sell a foreign currency spot, you transact today and the settlement (delivery) date is T+2.
 If you buy or sell a currency forward, you agree to a price today (the forward rate) and the settlement date or delivery date will be in the future depending on the maturity of the contract.
  - Most forward contracts are usually for the short term: 1 week, 1 month (1M), 3 months (3M).

## Slide 45  [layout: Title and Content]
 3 Exchange Rate Regimes
 The IMF classifies exchange rate arrangements based on actual (de facto) behavior, which often differs from officially announced arrangements.
 Hard Pegs
 No separate legal tender: Dollarization (Ecuador, El Salvador) or monetary union (Eurozone)
 Currency board: Domestic currency fully backed by foreign reserves at fixed rate (Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate System: HKD 7.80/USD since 1983)
 Advantages: Maximum credibility, eliminates exchange rate risk for trade;
 Disadvantages: Loss of monetary policy independence and lender-of-last-resort function

## Slide 46  [layout: Title and Content]
 3 Exchange Rate Regimes
 Soft Pegs
 Conventional peg: Rate maintained within ±1% of announced central rate (e.g., many Gulf states pegged to USD)
 Crawling peg: Rate adjusted at a predetermined rate (used by some EM economies to control inflation)
 Stabilized arrangement: Managed to stay within a narrow band without a formal commitment
 Retain some monetary policy flexibility but subject to speculative attack when fundamentals diverge from peg

## Slide 47  [layout: Title and Content]
 3 Exchange Rate Regimes
 Floating Arrangements
 Managed float: Authorities influence exchange rate without a specific target;
 interventions are discretionary based on balance of payments, reserves, and market conditions
 Freely floating: Rate determined by market supply and demand; interventions only to moderate excessive volatility

## Slide 48  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Impossible Trinity (Trilemma)
 A fundamental constraint in open-economy macroeconomics—the Mundell-Fleming trilemma—states that a country cannot simultaneously maintain all three of:
  - A fixed exchange rate
  - Free capital mobility
  - Independent monetary policy
 Only two can coexist. Regime choices illustrate the trade-offs:
  - Eurozone: Fixed rates + free capital mobility → surrendered monetary policy
  - Hong Kong SAR: Fixed rate + free capital → no independent monetary policy; HIBOR tracks Fed Funds Rate
  - China (historically): Fixed rate + monetary independence → imposed capital controls
  - US/UK/Japan: Free float + free capital → retain full monetary independence

## Slide 52  [layout: Title and Content]
 The Currency Carry Trade
 Hence investors like to do the currency carry trade.
 Borrow low-interest rate currencies (like the JPY) and invest the proceeds in high-interest rate currencies (like the AUD) and do not hedge your position.
  - If the AUD depreciates (as predicted by PPP, IRP), you will lose the additional AUD interest you earned.
  - But if the AUD does not depreciate against the JPY, you will earn the difference between the AUD and the JPY interest rates.
  - Better yet, if the AUD appreciates, you will earn both the interest rate difference and the appreciation of the AUD.
 Many academic studies (e.g. Lustig, Verdelhan, 2007) show that the carry trade indeed earns positive returns.
  - This is weird since high-interest rate currencies should depreciate according to many parity conditions.

## Slide 54  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why does carry trade work?
 No one has a good explanation on why the carry trade works. But there are some reasons suggested.
 Equity risk.
  - Carry trade portfolio risk could be related to equity risk. Lustig, Roussanov, & Verdelhan (2012) show that high-interest rate currencies returns are positively correlated with equity returns.
 Crash risk.
  - Empirical work shows carry trade portfolios earn small positive returns most of the time but occasionally suffer large, sudden drawdowns.
  - Carry tends to lose money in “risk‑off” episodes when volatility and measures like the VIX spike, because investors reduce leverage and unwind positions at the same time.

## Slide 57  [layout: Title and Content]
 Safe-Haven Currencies and Global Risk Sentiment
 A safe-haven currency appreciates (or at minimum maintains value) during periods of heightened global risk aversion and financial stress.
  - USD: Primary safe haven; benefits from flight to dollar liquidity; reserve currency status creates structural dollar demand during stress
  - JPY: Appreciates during risk-off as carry trade unwinds (Japanese investors repatriate foreign investments)
  - CHF: Switzerland's current account surplus, political neutrality, and liquid capital markets support safe-haven status
 Risk-on (VIX < 15): Investors sell safe-haven currencies (JPY, CHF), buy higher-yielding commodity currencies (AUD, NZD, CAD) and EM currencies; carry trades perform well
 Risk-off (VIX > 25-30): Rapid capital flows into USD and JPY; carry trades unwind; large negative tail returns for carry strategies

## Slide 58  [layout: Title and Content]
 Other Currency Strategies
 Currency momentum: buying past winner currencies and selling past loser currencies
  - Generates a large and significant cross-sectional spread of up to 10% per annum between past winner and loser portfolios over the period 1976–2010, covering more than 40 currencies
  - Not highly correlated with the carry trade, providing diversification
 Currency value strategies buy undervalued currencies (those trading below PPP) and sell overvalued ones.
  - While returns are lower and more variable than carry, value provides diversification against carry crash risk.
  - The combination of carry, value, and momentum generates substantially higher Sharpe ratios than any single strategy alone

## Slide 61  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
  - Exchange Rate Determination Theories:
    - Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): Exchange rates adjust to equalize prices of identical goods across countries (e.g., Big Mac Index).
    - Interest Rate Parity (IRP): Forward rates must offset interest rate differentials to prevent covered interest arbitrage.
  - The Carry Trade Anomaly:
    - Strategy: Borrow low-interest currency (JPY), invest in high-interest currency (AUD), unhedged.
    - Violates Uncovered IRP (which predicts high-rate currencies should depreciate).
    - Generates positive avg returns but suffers from crash risk (large losses during "risk-off" episodes/VIX spikes).
