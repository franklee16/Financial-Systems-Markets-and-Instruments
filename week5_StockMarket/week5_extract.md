## Slide 3  [layout: Blank]
 What Are Stocks?
 Stocks represent a piece of ownership in a corporation
 Shareholders are the legal owners of a corporation
  - have a right to share in the firm’s profits (e.g., through dividends although not guaranteed)
  - are residual claimants (paid only after all other claims are satisfied in the event of bankruptcy or liquidation)
  - have limited liability (lose only the amount invested in the stock)
  - control the firm’s activities indirectly by exercising their voting rights (e.g., to elect board of directors)

## Slide 4  [layout: 1_Title and Content]
 Common vs Preferred Stock
 Common stock is the fundamental ownership claim in a public or private corporation
  - Dividends are discretionary, and are thus not guaranteed
  - Common stockholders have the lowest priority in the event of bankruptcy and liquidation (that is a residual claim)
 Preferred stock: stock with priority over common stocks in payment of dividends and in liquidation
  - Generally has fixed dividends that are paid quarterly
  - Generally does not have voting rights unless dividend payments are missed
  - Nonparticipating: dividend is fixed regardless of any increase or decrease in the issuing firm’s profits
  - Cumulative: Any missed dividend payments go into arrears and must be made up before any common stock dividends can be paid

## Slide 5  [layout: Title and Content]
 Shareholders Meetings and Votes
 Publicly traded companies hold annual (or sometimes special) shareholder meetings (also called AGMs — Annual General Meetings) to vote on important issues
 Common voting items include:
  - Election of board directors
  - Approval of executive compensation (say-on-pay votes)
  - Approval of mergers, acquisitions, or major transactions
  - Ratification of auditors
  - Shareholder proposals (e.g., on environmental, social, governance — ESG — issues, or other changes)
 All shareholders have the right to attend the annual meeting and cast their votes directly or indirectly via proxy voting
  - Proxy voting: a written authorization for someone else to vote your shares

## Slide 6  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why Proxy Voting Matters?
 Since most individual and institutional shareholders cannot or do not attend in person (meetings can be virtual, hybrid, or in-person), proxy voting is the standard method — over 95% of votes in large public companies are cast by proxy.

## Slide 7  [layout: Title and Content]
 Proxy Contest and Shareholder Activism
 A proxy contest (also called a proxy fight or proxy battle) is a shareholder-driven campaign to gain influence or control over a company by soliciting votes (proxies) from other shareholders
  - usually in opposition to the company's existing management or board of directors
 Key Features of a Proxy Contest:
  - Triggered by dissatisfaction — Activists believe the company is underperforming, has poor governance, misallocated capital, or needs strategic change (e.g., sell the company, spin off divisions, replace CEO, cut costs)
  - Main goal — Usually to replace some or all board members with the activists' own nominees (a "board contest") or push through specific proposals

## Slide 10  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Activism Campaigns
 1. Project VAMPIRE (2003): Webb launched this initiative to combat "Value And Minority Protection Impaired by Rapacious Executives," opposing corporate resolutions that allowed companies to issue up to 20% new shares for cash without pre-emptive rights for existing shareholders.
 He argued this enabled controlling shareholders to dilute minority stakes unfairly. Through webb-site.com, Webb mobilized investors and lobbied regulators, eventually influencing calls to reduce the limit to 5% without a shareholder vote.
 2. The Enigma Network Exposé (2017): Webb revealed a web of 50 interconnected Hong Kong-listed companies – including brokers, construction firms, and even an umbrella manufacturer – linked by cross-shareholdings and common owners that artificially propped up stock prices.
 The report warned investors to avoid these stocks due to potential manipulation harming small shareholders. The exposé triggered immediate market turmoil: 38 stocks plunged, some by over 90%, wiping out billions in value.

## Slide 12  [layout: Blank]
 Primary Stock Markets
 Primary markets are markets in which corporations raise funds through new issues of stock, most of the time through investment banks
 Investment banks either act as distribution agents in best efforts underwriting, or as principals in firm commitment underwriting
  - For best effort, underwriter acts as an agent to sell the shares on behalf of the company, using their "best efforts" to market and sell them
  - For firm commitment, the underwriter buys the entire issue from the company at a discounted price and then resells it to the public. The underwriter assumes full ownership and risk
 Sometimes a syndicate (a group of investment banks) is formed to issue stock
  - the lead underwriter is called the originating house
  - spreading the risk among all the participants in the syndicate

## Slide 13  [layout: Blank]
 Equity Issuance
 An initial public offering (IPO) is the first public issue of financial instruments by a firm
  - Raise money to fund operations and provide liquidity for existing shareholders to sell shares
 A seasoned equity offering (SEO) is the sale of additional securities by a firm whose securities are already publicly traded
 Shelf registration allows firms to offer multiple issues of stock over a two-year period with only one registration statement
  - allows a company to adjust the timing of the sales to take advantage of more favorable market conditions should they arise

## Slide 23  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why are IPOs Underpriced?
 Winner’s curse
  - Informed investors will apply only if IPO is underpriced.
  - Uninformed investors know that they will get IPO shares only when informed investors don’t want to invest. So, the uninformed never want to participate in IPOs.
  - the firm needs to raise capital from both informed and uninformed, then IPOs on average have to be underpriced in order to get the uninformed to apply.
 Bandwagon hypothesis
  - Firm underprices to get first few investors to participate.
  - Subsequent investors blindly follow the first few investors. A story about information cascades.
  - Generates more demand for IPO  more underpricing.

## Slide 24  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why are IPOs underpriced?
 3.   Investment bank conflicts of interests
  - Banks have superior information and have power to underprice IPOs. They allocate IPOs to their favorite clients and expect future favors from those clients.
  - Evidence: for hot issues, underwriters set up personal brokerage accounts for venture capitalists and the executives of issuing firms in order to allocate hot IPOs to them.
 Lawsuit/Litigation avoidance
  - People who made money are less like to examine the prospectus for false statements and less likely to sue the firms/IB when the stocks underperformed later.
 5.    Irrational investors
  - Overoptimistic investors pay too much for the IPO on the first day of trading. Consistent with evidence of poor future long run performance.

## Slide 27  [layout: Title and Content]
 Why Long-run Underperformance?
 Convergence of opinion
  - Only optimistic investors buy on the first day. Pessimistic investors cannot short. Subsequently, the views of the pessimists get incorporated and the price drops.
 Passing of a fad
  - Investors slowly realize the potential of the firm was overstated. E.g., the technology bubble.
 Firms timing the IPO market
  - Firms try to time the market and do an IPO during times when their equity is the most overvalued.
 IPO as lottery
  - Investors are willing to pay high price hoping the stock will become the next Superstar with huge payoff (think about Google, Facebook etc.)

## Slide 32  [layout: Blank]
 Trading on NYSE vs. NASDAQ
 The NYSE (New York Stock Exchange) and NASDAQ differ significantly in trading mechanisms, structure, and how orders are executed
 Trading occurs at a specific place on the floor of the NYSE exchange called a trading post
 Each stock has a special market maker called a specialist (old) or Designated Market Maker (new) that maintains liquidity for the stock at all times
  - DDMs are responsible for stabilizing order flows and prices in turbulent market environment
 NASDAQ is primarily a dealer market where multiple dealers act as market makers for a particular stock
  - Investors are not buying from and selling to one another directly, but through a dealer
  [NOTES: DMM: https://www.nyse.com/market-model; https://www.nyse.com/publicdocs/nyse/listing/fact_sheet_dmm.pdf]

## Slide 34  [layout: Blank]
 Different Types of Orders
 Market orders and limit orders are two of the most common ways to place buy or sell instructions for securities through a broker.
 They differ primarily in price control, execution speed, and certainty of filling the trade.
 Market Order Example:
 You want to buy 100 shares of AAPL right now. You place a market buy order → it executes instantly at whatever the current ask price is (e.g., $225.40, even if you saw $225.10 seconds ago).
 Limit Order Example:
 You only want to buy AAPL if it drops to $220 or lower. You place a limit buy order at $220 → it only executes if sellers bring the price down to $220 (or better, like $219.50).
 If it never hits $220, no trade happens.

## Slide 35  [layout: Blank]
 Market Order vs Limit Order
  [TABLE]
    | Aspect | Market Order | Limit Order |
    | Definition | An order to buy or sell immediately at the best available current market price. | An order to buy or sell only at a specific price (or better) that you set. |
    | Execution | Executes almost immediately (or as soon as possible) during market hours. | Executes only if the market reaches your specified price (or better); may not execute at all. |
    | Price Guarantee | No price guarantee — you get whatever the current market offers (which could be worse than expected in volatile conditions). | Yes — you won't pay more (for buys) or receive less (for sells) than your limit price. |
    | Execution Guarantee | High — almost always fills quickly unless extremely illiquid. | No guarantee — if the price never hits your limit, the order won't fill (it may partially fill). |
    | Speed | Very fast — prioritizes immediacy. | Can be immediate (if price is already met) or take time/days/never fill. |
    | Best For | - Highly liquid stocks/ETFs - When you need to enter or exit a position right away - Stable, low-volatility markets - Not concerned about small price slippage | - Wanting to control the exact price - Volatile or fast-moving markets - Setting a target entry/exit price - Avoiding paying too much or selling too low |

## Slide 40  [layout: Blank]
 Calculating Stock Returns
 The holding-period returns on a stock over one period (Rt) can be divided into capital gains and dividends:
    - Pt = stock price at time t
    - Dt = dividends paid over time t – 1 to t
    - (Pt – Pt – 1) / Pt – 1 = capital gain over time t – 1 to t
    - Dt / Pt – 1 = return from dividends paid over time t – 1 to t
  - Note this is pre-tax return. To calculate after-tax return you need to multiple by (1- tax rate), which is usually different for capital gains versus dividends

## Slide 55  [layout: Blank]
 US Stock Market Regulations
 The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is the primary regulator of US stock markets
  - Promote full disclosure of information on securities and ensure fair treatment of investors
  - Enforce Securities Act of 1933 and Securities Exchange Act of 1934
  - Prosecute Illegal Insider Trading
 The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) is the regulator for all U.S. securities firms
  - FINRA overseeing brokers and dealers,
  - examining securities firms and promulgating rules,
  - enforcing federal securities laws and conducting dispute arbitration

  - Safeguard the interests of the investing public by ensuring fair treatment, transparency, and adequate disclosure.
 Maintaining Fair, Orderly, and Efficient Markets
 Licensing and Supervision of Intermediaries
 Enforcement and Discipline
  - Investigate suspected breaches of securities laws.
 Promoting Market Development and Education
  - Facilitate and encourage the orderly growth and international competitiveness of Hong Kong's financial markets.

 Regulation of Products and Activities
  - Authorize and oversee collective investment schemes (e.g., mutual funds, unit trusts).

## Slide 58  [layout: 2_Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 1-
 Stocks represent ownership (common stock) with voting rights & residual claim. Preferred stock has fixed dividends & priority but usually no vote.
 Primary Market:
  - Firms raise capital via investment banks (firm commitment vs. best efforts).
  - IPO Anomalies: Significant underpricing (high first-day returns) followed by long-run underperformance (3-5 years).
  - Reasons: Winner's curse, bank conflicts, market timing.
 Secondary Market Trading:
  - NYSE: Auction market with designated market makers.
  - NASDAQ: Dealer market with multiple market makers.
  - Order Types: Market orders (immediate execution) vs. Limit orders (price control).
  - Short Selling: Betting on price decline; borrowing shares. High short interest predicts negative returns.

## Slide 59  [layout: 2_Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 Stock Returns:
  - Total Return = Capital Gains + Dividends.
 Stock Market Indexes:
  - Price-Weighted (DJIA): Higher-priced stocks have more influence.
  - Market-Cap Weighted (S&P 500): Larger companies have more influence.
  - Indexes are leading economic indicators.
 Regulation:
  - SEC (US) / SFC (HK): Ensure full disclosure, fair trading, and prosecute insider trading.
