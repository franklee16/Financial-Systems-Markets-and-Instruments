## Slide 7  [layout: Title and Content]
 Net Asset Value (NAV)
 NAV is the current market value of the investment company’s assets, minus its liabilities (e.g., fund expenses), divided by the number of shares outstanding
 The market value of a fund’s assets is determined either by the market price for those assets if available, or if a price is not readily available, a fair value as determined in good faith (based on internal models and third-party appraisals)
  [IMAGE -> figures/slide07_img.png]

## Slide 8  [layout: Title and Content]
 NAV Calculation: Example
 Suppose today a mutual fund contains 1,000 shares of Walmart currently trading at $63.75, 1,000 shares of ExxonMobil currently trading at $86.00, and 1,400 shares of AT&T currently trading at $33.66. The mutual fund has no liabilities and 15,000 shares outstanding held by investors. Thus, today, the NAV of the fund’s shares is calculated as:
 NAV = (1,000*$63.75+1,000*$86+1,400*$33.66)/15,000 = $13.125
 If tomorrow Walmart ’s stock price decreases to $59.6, ExxonMobil’s price increases to $104, and AT&T’s price decreases to $20, the NAV (assuming the number of shares outstanding remains the same) would be:
 NAV =(1,000*$59.6+1,000*$104+1,400*$20)/15,000 = $12.77

## Slide 19  [layout: Title and Content]
 Mutual Fund Returns
 You can earn returns from your mutual funds in three ways:
  - Income Distribution — A fund may earn income in the form of dividends and interests on the securities in its portfolio. The fund then pays its shareholders nearly all of the income (minus disclosed expenses) it has earned in the form of dividends.
  - Capital Gains Distribution — The price of the securities a fund owns may increase. When a fund sells a security that has increased in price, the fund has a capital gain. At the end of the year, most funds distribute these capital gains (minus any capital losses) to investors.
  - Increased NAV — If the market value of a fund's portfolios increases after deduction of expenses and liabilities, the fund’s NAV also increases.
  [NOTES: With respect to dividend payments and capital gains distributions, funds usually will give you a choice: the fund can send you a check or other form of payment, or you can have your dividends or distributions reinvested in the fund to buy more shares (often without paying an additional sales load).]

## Slide 21  [layout: Title and Content]
 Costs of Investing in Mutual Funds: Fee Structure
  [TABLE]
    | Fee Structure | Definition |
    | Operating Expenses | Annual costs incurred by the mutual fund in operating portfolio, like admin expenses and advisory fees paid to the investment manager.  Range from 0.5% to 1.25% of the total assets |
    | Front-end Load | Commission or sales charge paid when purchasing shares It reduces the amount available to purchase fund shares |

## Slide 24  [layout: Title Only]
 Impacts of Costs on Investment Performance: Example
 Assume you have $1,000 to invest.
 The initial investment in Class A shares is $940 net of the front-end load. After four years, your portfolio will be worth:
 $940  (1+10%)4 = $1,376.25
 Class C shares allow you to invest the full $1,000, but your investment performance net of 12b-1 fees will be only 9.5%, and you will pay a 1% back-end load if you sell after four years. Your portfolio value after four years will be:
 $1,000  (1+9.5%)4 = $1,437.66
 After paying the back-end load fees, your portfolio value will be:
 $1,437.66  0.99 = $1,423.28
 Class C shares is the better choice if your horizon is four years.

## Slide 25  [layout: Title Only]
 Impacts of Costs on Investment Performance: Example
 With a 15-year horizon, the Class A shares will be worth:
 $940  (1+10%)15 = $3,926.61
 For the Class C shares, there is no back-end load in this case since the horizon is greater than five years. Therefore, the value of the Class C shares will be:
 $1,000  (1+9.5%)15 = $3,901.32
 With longer horizon, Class C shares is no longer the better choice. The effect of Class C's 0.5% annual 12b-1 fees accumulates over time and finally overwhelms the 6% (one time) front-end load charged to Class A shares

## Slide 36  [layout: Title and Content]
 Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)
 Similar to index funds, ETFs are generally designed to track a particular index (e.g., S&P 500)
 However, ETFs can be traded on stock exchanges throughout the day and are generally highly liquid
 Investors could use ETFs for different purposes
 Long-term asset allocation
    - Financial advisors use ETFs to build diversified portfolios for clients
 Intermediate-term sector rotation/tactical asset allocation
    - ETFs offer low-cost access to countries, sectors, and assets
 Short-term speculation
    - High liquidity and ease of trading allows investors to change position quickly
    - Investors can also short sell ETFs to express bearish view or take leveraged position in ETFs

## Slide 38  [layout: Title and Content]
 ETFs vs. Index Funds
 ETFs differ from index funds in the following ways:
  - ETF shares are traded on the exchanges intraday
    - Investors buy/sell ETF shares through brokers – and pay commissions on the trades
    - Shares are only redeemable from the fund itself in very large blocks
  - ETF price may differ from its NAV
    - ETF prices reflect the investor demand/supply in the secondary market
    - ETF NAV is based on the market value of underlying assets held by the ETF
    - Arbitrage help keep the market determined price of an ETF close to its net asset value

## Slide 42  [layout: Title and Content]
 What is Hedge Fund?
 A hedge fund is a partnership where investors pool money together, and a fund manager deploys the money in a variety of assets using sophisticated investment techniques
  - Hedge funds are only accessible to accredited and/or institutional investors
  - Hedge funds can use leverage, take short positions, and hold long/short positions in derivatives
  - They are less strictly regulated by SEC as opposed to mutual funds
 HFs seek absolute returns by exploiting investment opportunities while protecting principal from potential loss
  - Their investment objective is materially different from that of MF manager who is tied to an index benchmark
  [NOTES: Source: http://www.aima.org/en/knowledge_centre/education/aimas-roadmap-to-hedge-funds.cfm]

## Slide 53  [layout: Title and Content]
 Management fees on HFs are computed as a percent of assets under management and run between 1%-2%
 Performance (incentive) fees give fund managers a share of any positive returns earned above a benchmark
  - The average is 20%, but performance fees vary substantially depending on the HF (Steve Cohen’s SAC Capital charged performance fee of 50%)
  - A hurdle rate is a benchmark return that must be realized before a performance fee can be assessed
  - A high-water mark is when a manager does not receive a performance fee unless the NAV of the fund exceeds the highest NAV previously recorded

## Slide 56  [layout: Title and Content]
 Hedge Fund Fee Structure: Example
 A hedge fund has $1 million of assets under management. The fund follows a “2 and 20” fee structure with a hurdle rate of 5%.
 The fund earned 20% gross return this year. Calculate the total amount of fees paid to the fund manager.
 Performance fees are calculated on gross returns (not returns net of management fees).
 Since the portfolio generated a gross return of 20%, which is above the 5% hurdle rate, the fund managers are entitled to a performance fee.

## Slide 57  [layout: Title and Content]
 Hedge Fund Fee Structure: Example
 First, we calculate the management fees as $1,000,000 x 2% = $20,000.
 We then calculate dollar return above the hurdle rate as $1,000,000*(20%-5%) = $150,000. This is the dollar amount that the incentive fees will be charged against.
 Lastly, we calculate the performance fees as $150,000 x 20% = $30,000.
 Total fees paid to the fund managers are $20,000 + $30,000 = $50,000.

## Slide 67  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 Investment Companies: Pool investor funds for diversification, professional management & low minimums. Priced at NAV (Net Asset Value).
 Mutual Funds (Open-End):
  - Types: Money Market, Equity, Bond, Sector, International.
  - Returns: Income distributions, capital gains distributions, NAV appreciation.
  - Costs: Front/back-end loads, 12b-1 fees, expense ratios. Fees significantly impact long-term returns.
  - Active vs. Passive: Active funds try to beat benchmarks; Index funds (passive) replicate indexes with lower fees. Passive investing is rising globally.
 Closed-End Funds: Fixed shares, traded on exchanges at prices that can be at a premium or discount to NAV.

## Slide 68  [layout: Title and Content]
 Key Takeaways
 Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs):
  - Combine index fund features with intraday trading.
  - Arbitrage mechanism (creation/redemption) keeps market price close to NAV.
  - Low-cost, flexible tools for allocation, sector rotation, or speculation.
 Hedge Funds:
  - Private partnerships for accredited investors. Use leverage, shorting, derivatives.
  - Goal: Absolute returns (not benchmark-relative).
  - Fee Structure: "2 and 20" (2% management, 20% performance) with high-water mark.
  - Performance: Lower volatility & downside protection vs. S&P 500, but long-term absolute underperformance.
 Funds of Funds (FoFs): Invest in multiple hedge funds. Provide diversification but add two layers of fees.
